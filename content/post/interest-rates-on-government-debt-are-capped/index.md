Today, central governments may have to issue bonds to cover net spending,
but the central bank of the currency in which the debt is denominated has
long been able to control the price. With the advent of Real-Time Gross
Settlement (RTGS) systems and Central Securities Depositories (CSD), the
central bank has had to provide the liquidity needed to keep payment
and securities settlement systems functioning. That has given it an
increasingly direct influence over the cost of financing government issues.

Over the past few months, as demand for longer-term government debt has
weakened and yields have risen, some commentary, particularly within MMT
discussions, has suggested that the private market freely determines
the price of government debt, including the sub-sovereign debt issued
by Eurozone member states. Taken at face value, this implies that the
private sector can set those yields at whatever level it chooses.

This post explains why that isn't the case, why interest rates on
government debt have a cap, regardless of debt or deficit size, and
why that cap is tightest and easiest to engage at the short end.

As institutional arrangements stand, all government deficit spending,
across all types of governments, is covered by issuing bonds. Only
governments constrained in how many bonds they can issue end up being
limited in their deficit spending. This includes the UK's sub-sovereign
assemblies, the state governments of various federations, and even the
US federal government itself due to the infamous "debt ceiling". In
contrast, the UK central government has no explicit limitations.

What's interesting about the Eurosystem is that many member states have no
entrenched legislative restrictions on issuing government debt. They have
a treaty restriction on direct central bank financing, which has broadly
the same effect as the Federal Reserve's inability to lend directly to
the US federal government. France, for example, is a sovereign state but
a currency user within the Eurozone. Its government does not issue or
individually control the currency it spends, and it finances its deficit
through market borrowing, with parliamentary authorisation.

The national legislature sets the quantity and form of government
borrowing, and the settlement system caps the price. That cap on interest
rates on government bonds stems from the shift towards gross settlement
systems. Banks used to pay each other during the day and settle only
the net difference at the end of the day. That left each bank exposed
to the other's failure in the meantime. If a global financial crisis
hit in the morning and a bank went bust that day, its counterparties
could take a massive loss. The demand for certainty, and for money
to move faster within the day, has led to systems with immediate full
settlement. Payments become "gross", in other words. The catch is that
gross settlement requires substantial liquidity at the central bank.

The result is a Mexican standoff in which banks become "receipt-reactive"
[^7], leaving payments to the end of the day in the hope that another
bank will pay them first so they can recycle the liquidity.

To address this, central banks introduce liquidity facilities and
liquidity-saving mechanisms. Intraday credit and auto-collateralisation
facilities, in particular, depend on the availability of "high quality
collateral", with government bonds forming a particularly important
part of that collateral. Alongside the development of the wholesale repo
market and the slow withdrawal of Quantitative Easing (QE), government
bonds have become essentially liquid at no cost during the day and at
low cost overnight.

In effect, regulated banks have become money changers at the temple,
swapping one sort of money for another and charging a fee. It is no
longer tenable to argue that bonds "sterilise" liquidity in quantity
terms. In addition, many years of QE, involving substantial increases in
central-bank balances and commercial-bank deposits without a corresponding
or proportionate increase in consumer prices, make the argument that
monetary expansion automatically produces inflation indefensible. In the
recent Treasury bill consultation run by the UK Debt Management Office,
no respondent mentioned that expanding bank deposits has had or would
have any effect on inflation [^1]. Holding government paper is, of
course, routine banking practice and has been for generations.

The net result is that the central bank is committed to providing
ever-increasing liquidity support, particularly within the CSD [^2].
Even within the Eurosystem, the Correspondent Central Banking Model
and Home Central Bank liquidity provision tightly bind the national
central banks together [^3]. Removing or adjusting collateral limits
then carries significant systemic and political risk.

This evolution and entanglement means the interest rate on government
bonds is capped at a level that reflects the expected path of the base
rate, the collateral conditions and the regulatory treatment of the
intermediary's balance sheet: the so-called "all-in" cost.[^4] If the
yield on government bonds rises above that level, an eligible system
participant can buy the bonds and finance the purchase through central
bank facilities, using the bonds themselves as collateral. The difference
between the bond yield and the cost of financing the position provides
the intermediary's profit. Other participants have an incentive to do
the same, competing to acquire the bonds, bidding up their price and
driving down their yield. In essence, the government can borrow from the
central bank at a set rate, with the intermediary acting as a middleman
and charging a fee.[^5] That fee is subject to competitive compression.

The cap is tightest on bonds with maturities under twelve months,
which banks will hold directly. Longer bonds are also capped, since an
intermediary can repo them at the central bank, but the range is much
wider because the expected path of the base rate has a much greater
dispersion, making the
[haircut](https://www.icmagroup.org/market-practice-and-regulatory-policy/repo-and-collateral-markets/icma-ercc-publications/frequently-asked-questions-on-repo/21-what-is-a-haircut)
much larger and the risk much greater.[^8]

As the base rate rises, the discount to redemption value increases.
Through the [pull-to-redemption effect](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/p/pull-to-par.asp),
short-term government bonds with low haircuts can accrete enough value
through their remaining lifetime to cover the initial haircut, reducing
the "all-in" cost.

Any government wishing to cap its interest costs should move to short-term
paper [^6] as it limits the dispersion effect of the expected path over
the lifetime of the bond, engages the accretion effect and taps into the
multiple bid effect (with both banks and their customers bidding for
the bonds with effectively the same money). In the Eurosystem's case,
governments should use at least some intermediaries in other member
states to increase the entanglement effect and bring wider EU Treaty
rights and protections into play.

The private market cannot set government debt prices at will. Governments
may have to issue bonds to cover net spending, but RTGS and CSD have
forced central banks to supply liquidity using government bonds as
collateral. That caps the yield at an "all-in" cost, because any system
participant can step in as a middleman above it. The range is tightest
with short-term government bonds and widens with maturity, which is
why Warren Mosler’s three-month bills remain the way to keep interest
costs down.
